Americans, media and official messaging focus on different priorities during the Iran war

On February 28, the day the United States launched strikes on Iran, President Donald Trump said the operation was intended to eliminate “imminent threats from the Iranian regime.” He repeatedly emphasized that Iran “can never have a nuclear weapon.”

But within days, the public conversation began to shift away from that justification.

Search data show that Americans quickly moved from focusing on nuclear threats and the war itself to searching for information about gas prices and the risk of escalation. News coverage remained centered on military developments. Official messaging from the White House emphasized Iran as a strategic actor rather than economic consequences or escalation risks.

An analysis of Google Trends search data, Media Cloud coverage patterns, and social media posts from President Trump and the White House between February 1 and April 8 reveals a clear gap between what Americans were concerned about, what journalists emphasized, and what officials chose to highlight. The timeline spans the weeks before the strikes through the first ceasefire agreement between the United States and Iran.

Together, the data suggest that the public, the media, and official messaging framed the conflict in fundamentally different ways during the first phase of the war.

Search interest data show that Americans initially paid close attention to the war immediately after the strikes. That attention declined quickly. Within days, searches related to gas and oil prices rose sharply and remained consistently higher than searches related to the war for most of the conflict period.
Searches related to escalation risks also increased immediately after the strikes, reflecting concern that the conflict might expand beyond its initial scope.

Both public and media attention to nuclear risks declined rapidly after the first days of the conflict, even though preventing Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons had been presented as a central justification for the strikes.

For many Americans, the war appeared first as a national security or geopolitical event and then increasingly as an economic concern affecting daily life.
Ellen Tannor Richardson, a graduate student and mother of four in Alexandria, Virginia, said she immediately noticed rising fuel costs.

“I could clearly feel gas prices going up,” she said. “But I try not to pay too much attention to Trump. I think he’s completely crazy. Just a joke.”
Her reaction reflects how some Americans interpreted the conflict less through military developments themselves and more through its impact on household affordability.

News coverage followed a different trajectory.

Media Cloud data show that reporting remained consistently focused on the war itself throughout the conflict period, while coverage related to gas and oil prices remained secondary compared with military developments.

In the early phase of a war, journalists often rely heavily on official military sources. That reliance naturally shifts coverage toward national security risks and escalation scenarios, said Wendy Melillo, associate professor of journalism at American University.

“Reporting on military escalation and the threat of nuclear weapons being used is necessary since such decisions affect every living person on the planet,” she said.

Jane Hall, a professor of journalism at American University, said the early emphasis on national security risks also reflected the surprise surrounding the conflict itself.

“Most news organizations were shocked and surprised by this war,” she said.

The sudden shift in U.S. policy created uncertainty about whether the conflict might expand and whether U.S. troops might become involved, which helped explain why coverage initially centered on escalation risks rather than economic consequences.

Official messaging followed yet another pattern.

Social media posts from President Trump and the White House emphasized Iran itself far more than escalation risks or economic consequences. Mentions of gas prices were rare. References to escalation risks were almost entirely absent.

Despite the administration’s repeated emphasis on preventing Iran from obtaining a nuclear weapon on the day of the strikes, references to nuclear risks quickly declined in both public attention and official messaging in the weeks that followed.

Governments often avoid escalation language during crises because such rhetoric can intensify tensions and reduce diplomatic flexibility, said Rhonda Zaharna, a professor of strategic communication at American University.

“Official diplomacy seeks to deescalate tensions during crisis situations,” she said.

Avoiding escalation language can also help reduce market volatility and preserve room for negotiation during uncertain international confrontations.

Together, these differences suggest that Americans, journalists, and policymakers interpreted the war through distinct priorities shaped by their institutional roles.

For the public, the conflict increasingly became a story about affordability and everyday economic pressure. For journalists, it remained primarily a national security story. For policymakers, it was framed as a strategic confrontation centered on Iran itself rather than on escalation risks or domestic economic consequences.

Although President Trump justified the strikes by warning about the threat posed by Iran’s nuclear ambitions, public attention, media coverage, and official messaging all moved in different directions in the weeks that followed.

Search behavior shifted mainly toward rising fuel costs. News coverage remained centered on military developments. White House messaging emphasized Iran itself while rarely returning to the nuclear justification that helped explain the decision to strike in the first place.

More than a month after the conflict began, the issue that framed the opening announcement of the war no longer defined the public conversation surrounding it.
